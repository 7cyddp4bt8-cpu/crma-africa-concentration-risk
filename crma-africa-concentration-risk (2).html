I — The precedent

Germany did not build this dependency through negligence

Germany today pays some of the highest industrial energy prices in the world. The cause is not a market failure — it is the predictable consequence of a deliberate strategic choice: decades of concentrated dependency on a single supplier, without a hedge, in the confident belief that stability was structural rather than contingent. The EU watched this unfold and then, in the same legislative cycle that produced REPowerEU, quietly built an identical architecture for critical minerals. The Critical Raw Materials Act legislates against concentration risk. The partnerships signed under it systematically rebuild it. This brief uses the Act's own risk framework to prove the contradiction — and to propose three fixes before the July 2026 mandatory review closes the window.

Germany built its Russian gas dependency through Wandel durch Handel — a coherent, explicitly argued strategic logic of change through trade. The argument had serious intellectual backing, political consensus across decades, and institutional reinforcement through Nordstream 1 and 2. It was catastrophically wrong. When it failed, it failed completely — no hedge, no fallback, no buffer that mattered. Four characteristics made the failure total: no liquid spot market for alternatives, no stored buffer, no contracted alternatives, and infrastructure lock-in optimised for a single supplier. The EU's African mineral position shares all four.

"The EU has legislated against concentration risk and then systematically built it, without a hedge. The CRMA's African architecture is not a diversification strategy but a dependency substitution — and its own risk framework proves it."

The CRMA's own benchmark prohibits more than 65% reliance on any single country for any strategic raw material. Of seven key minerals assessed here, five already breach this threshold. The DRC exercised exactly this leverage in February 2025 with a cobalt export ban, extended twice, cutting 2026 quota volumes to roughly half of normal monthly levels. The architecture failed its own stress test before it was fully built.

II — The comparison

China didn't secure minerals. It bought the processing relationship, the infrastructure, and the political dependency simultaneously.

The EU walks into African capitals with ESG conditionality, governance benchmarks, and supply chain due diligence requirements — while simultaneously maintaining an MoU with Rwanda that the UN says is sourcing conflict minerals from DRC. The moral high ground is not just unconvincing. It is empirically false. And African negotiators know it, which is precisely why they keep their options open with China. The argument is not that ESG doesn't matter. It is that credibility precedes conditionality. You cannot demand governance standards from a partner while your own partnership architecture has a documented due diligence failure in the second row of your strategic project list.

China operated on a three-layer model that the EU has not matched at any layer:

Layer 01
Mining equity, not offtake
Chinese firms hold stakes in 18 of 26 DRC cobalt assets and 80% of African lithium mines. Not agreements to buy — ownership. Skin in the game from day one, creating shared upside and political relationships that outlast any government.
Layer 02
Processing inside Africa
Zhejiang Huayou's $400m lithium sulphate plant in Zimbabwe. CMOC refinery investments in DRC. China built the downstream inside the country — creating jobs, tax revenue, and political dependency simultaneously. The EU builds offtake agreements and calls it partnership.
Layer 03
Infrastructure as leverage
Belt and Road corridors, ports, railways — all designed to route mineral flows toward China. The Tanzania-Zambia railway connects to Dar es Salaam. The EU's Lobito Corridor counter is one corridor against two decades of Chinese infrastructure investment.

China's model works for China. But it is now generating exactly the backlash the EU could exploit — debt traps, environmental degradation, labour practices, governance conflicts. African civil society and governments are increasingly vocal about Chinese extractivism. The DRC mining minister said it directly: "colonial-era pit to port must end." He was not only talking about Europe.

The red line
The EU must not replicate China's extraction-first model. But it must match China's capital commitment and infrastructure seriousness — while differentiating on the one dimension China structurally cannot offer: genuine processing partnerships with African equity participation, rule-of-law frameworks, and ESG standards that withstand scrutiny. That combination makes the EU first choice. But only if the actions match the rhetoric. Right now they do not. Capital first. Conditionality second. Equity before lectures.

III — The mechanism

Dependency cannot always be avoided. What can be designed is mutually assured stability.

The green transition is not a one-time procurement exercise. It is a Kaizen-dependent industrial transformation — a continuous, decades-long process requiring predictable, high-specification mineral inputs at scale. Toyota's just-in-time system only functions because supplier relationships are treated as long-term equity partnerships. Toyota co-invests in supplier capability and takes on supplier risk in downturns. In return it gets reliability that no spot market can provide. The EU is attempting to run a Kaizen-dependent industrial strategy on a spot-market diplomatic model. Memoranda of Understanding are the supply chain equivalent of buying parts on the open market the morning the factory needs them.

The question is therefore not how to eliminate dependency — the minerals are where they are — but how to structure it so that both sides have something to lose if the relationship breaks. Five instruments achieve this:

Instrument 01
Equity co-investment — the Toyota model formalised
When the EIB holds equity in a Zambian cobalt refinery, both sides have capital at risk. When Zambian state mining company ZCCM holds equity in the downstream offtake chain into Europe, Zambia has a financial interest in European battery production succeeding. Both sides now have something to lose. The relationship becomes self-enforcing in a way no MoU can replicate.
Instrument 02
Processing corridors as reciprocal lock-in
The Lobito Corridor is currently designed as European extraction infrastructure. Redesign it as African industrial infrastructure that connects to European markets. Build processing facilities along the corridor, not at the European end. Then the corridor's economic value to Angola, DRC and Zambia depends on European market access — and EU supply security depends on corridor stability. Neither side can defect without destroying value they own.
Instrument 03
Preferential access tied to processing — the trade instrument
Zero tariff on battery-grade lithium sulphate from Zimbabwe; standard tariff on raw spodumene. That single instrument redirects the processing investment incentive without renegotiating a single MoU. Zimbabwe is already banning raw lithium exports unilaterally. The EU could offer this proactively and own the relationship.
Instrument 04
Strategic stockpile as negotiating stability
90 days of strategic cobalt reserves makes a DRC export ban an inconvenience not a crisis. That buffer changes the negotiating dynamic — the EU is no longer a desperate buyer in a seller's market the moment supply tightens. The EU's gas storage regulation mandates 80% fill levels by November each year. Exactly this instrument applied to minerals.
Instrument 05
AU-level framework replacing bilaterals
African states with divergent interests would not coordinate an export restriction uniformly — that divergence is the natural hedge the bilateral architecture forfeits. One multilateral instrument with all African partners simultaneously is both cheaper to maintain and structurally more resilient than five separate bilateral relationships each carrying full single-country exposure.

IV — The investment thesis

This is not a cost. It is a first-mover position in the asset class of the next industrial era.

Africa holds 48% of global cobalt reserves, 43% of manganese, 24% of bauxite, and meaningful shares of copper, graphite, and platinum-group metals. These are not legacy commodities. They are the input stack for every technology the EU's industrial future depends on — electric vehicles, wind turbines, battery storage, defence systems, AI data centres. The IEA projects supply shortfalls in copper and lithium within a decade. The demand curve is structurally upward and the supply curve cannot keep pace.

An EU institutional investor that takes equity positions in African processing infrastructure today is not spending aid money. It is buying exposure to the single most strategically valuable asset class of the next industrial era, at a price that reflects current African political risk discounts rather than future scarcity premiums. When those scarcity premiums materialise — and the IEA's own data says they will — the returns flow back to European institutional balance sheets while the processing capacity stays in Africa, creating the jobs, tax revenue and industrial development that makes the relationship politically sustainable on both sides. The profits trickle back. The manufacturing capability stays at source.

The Marshall Plan was not altruism. America invested in European reconstruction because a prosperous, stable, industrialising Europe was the single best long-run customer for American exports, the most reliable buffer against Soviet expansion, and the highest-returning capital allocation available in 1948. Seventy-five years later, that investment still pays dividends — in trade relationships, in alliance structures, in the kind of deep institutional friendship that persists even when individual leaders make threats about annexing allied territory. The relationship survived because it was built on genuine mutual prosperity, not transactional extraction. The EU has the same opportunity in Africa today. The minerals are the entry point. The industrial partnership is the product. If everyone loses, no one gains. That is not idealism. It is the most hardheaded strategic calculation available.

Beyond supply security, every processing facility built in Zambia, every battery component plant in the DRC corridor, every rare earth refinery in Namibia is also a reason for a skilled African engineer not to migrate to Germany. The investment thesis, the supply security thesis, and the migration management thesis converge on exactly the same intervention — African industrial development at the source of the minerals Europe needs. This is not a foreign policy concession. It is a portfolio allocation decision that happens to solve three geopolitical problems simultaneously.

VI — De lege ferenda · July 2026 CRMA review

What a hedged architecture looks like — three proposals

The July 2026 mandatory CRMA review is the first real legislative window. African multi-alignment is not a threat to be managed — it is a negotiating reality to be met with substance. The South Africa CTIP is the proof of concept. The question for the review is whether it becomes the template or remains the exception.

Proposal 01
Apply Article 5 concentration risk methodology formally to external partnerships
Before any bilateral CRM agreement is signed, the Commission must publish a mandatory concentration risk assessment using the same criteria applied to domestic strategic materials. If the partnership deepens single-country exposure beyond 65%, it requires a compensating hedge instrument as a condition of proceeding. The legislation already contains the methodology. Apply it consistently — to China and to Africa equally. Inconsistent application of your own risk framework is not a policy. It is a liability.
Proposal 02
Replace fragmented bilaterals with a single AU-EU Critical Minerals Framework Agreement
One multilateral instrument with all African partners simultaneously eliminates single-country political risk. African states with divergent interests would not coordinate an export restriction uniformly — that divergence is the natural hedge the bilateral architecture forfeits. The EU's gas storage regulation mandates 80% fill levels by November each year — exactly this instrument applied to minerals produces a strategic stockpile obligation and a multilateral fallback. Both are cheap to build in peacetime and unavailable during a supply shock.
Proposal 03
Mandate EIB first-loss co-investment in African processing infrastructure
To receive EU strategic project status, African partnerships must demonstrate a minimum 25% of capital expenditure allocated to in-country processing, with the EIB holding a subordinated equity position — losing money before African partners do if the project underperforms. Shared downside is what aligns incentives. Political agreements do not. This is the skin-in-the-game mechanism that transforms a transactional supply relationship into a structurally stable industrial partnership — the difference between Nordstream and a genuine joint venture. The returns flow back to Europe. The manufacturing capability stays at source. Both sides win over the timescale that matters.

VII — Conclusion

The window is open. It will not stay open.

The CRMA is a sophisticated piece of supply chain law that applies rigorous risk thinking domestically and abandons it at the border. The African partnership architecture is not a diversification strategy — it is a dependency substitution, built without a hedge, in a domain where the underlying assets are politically capturable, physically irreplaceable, and have no liquid secondary market. That is a worse risk profile than anything a serious risk framework would tolerate.

Germany's experience is not a cautionary tale about Russia. It is a cautionary tale about what happens when stability assumptions substitute for hedge instruments — in any domain, with any counterparty. The DRC's February 2025 export ban is the first stress test. It will not be the last. Three of five African partnerships are already showing structural fragility within two years of signing.

The EU is not being asked to solve Africa's development problem. It is being offered first-mover access to the processing infrastructure of the next industrial era, at a discount that will not persist. The minerals are there. The demand is coming. The partnership model that works — equity co-investment, processing infrastructure at source, shared downside, multilateral architecture — is already proven in the South Africa CTIP. The only question is whether it becomes the template or the exception.

Germany's energy crisis was a failure of strategic imagination. This time the imagination failure would be voluntary. If everyone loses, no one gains.
